A breakdown at a Dubai port or anchorage rarely happens at a convenient time. The vessel may be on a tight cargo window, the berth may be needed for the next ship, and the crew is already managing the immediate safety response. The first hour sets the tone for everything that follows.

This guide is a practical first-response sequence for ship managers, superintendents and chief engineers facing an unexpected repair requirement in Dubai.

## 1. Secure the vessel and the people

Before anything else, make the situation safe. Isolate the affected system, follow the vessel's safety management procedures, and confirm that the crew is accounted for. If the fault affects propulsion, steering, power or cargo handling, notify the port and the relevant authorities as required.

Do not let commercial pressure push the crew into an unsafe workaround. A temporary fix that creates a fire, pollution or injury risk is not a fix.

## 2. Document the fault

The quality of the repair response depends on the quality of the information. Before equipment is opened up or settings are changed, capture:

- Alarm messages and fault codes
- Photos of the affected area and nameplates
- Pressure, temperature, voltage or current readings
- What the vessel was doing when the fault appeared
- Any recent maintenance, fuel change or configuration change
- What has already been checked or attempted

This record helps the technical team prepare and prevents repeated diagnosis.

## 3. Call the operations desk with the essentials

A useful first call includes:

- Vessel name, IMO and local agent
- Port, berth or anchorage
- Equipment maker, model and serial number
- Fault description and current status
- Required working window
- A contact on board and a contact ashore

If parts may be needed, include part numbers or clear photos of the nameplate and the failed component. If the vessel is at anchorage, say so early; access and weather may affect mobilisation.

## 4. Agree the immediate plan

The repair team should confirm:

- Who is attending and when
- What access and permits are required
- What the vessel should prepare before arrival
- Whether a temporary measure is safe and appropriate
- What information is still missing

For a major failure, the plan may be a two-stage response: stabilise the vessel and restore safe operation, then complete a permanent repair when parts or a yard are available.

## 5. Deal with parts early

Parts are the most common cause of emergency repair delay. The provider should confirm the sourcing route, lead time and whether an approved alternative is acceptable. For Caterpillar, Yanmar and other major makers, serial and part numbers are essential.

If the part must be imported, start the clearance and logistics process in parallel with the technical work. Do not wait for the repair to be fully diagnosed before checking availability.

## 6. Involve class, flag and port when needed

Repairs that affect class or statutory items should be discussed with the class surveyor as early as possible. The surveyor can confirm the acceptable repair method and the documentation required. Port authorities may also need to approve hot work, diving, pollution response or work at anchorage.

The vessel's agent is often the practical link for port coordination. Keep them informed of the plan and timing.

## 7. Manage the working window

Emergency work often happens outside normal hours. Agree shift patterns, supervision, rest arrangements and reporting. For work that extends beyond a shift, request a written progress update and photographs at agreed intervals.

If the repair is likely to affect the vessel's schedule, tell the charterer and the operations team early. A realistic revised estimate is more useful than an optimistic one that keeps slipping.

## 8. Close out with evidence

At completion, collect:

- A completion report
- Test results and operating readings
- Photographs of the repair
- Parts used and their identification
- Any temporary measures and their limitations
- Class or survey documentation where applicable
- Warranty or follow-up terms

This record protects the vessel during inspections and provides a baseline for future maintenance.

## What not to do

- Do not authorise work without a clear scope and cost basis.
- Do not let a temporary repair become permanent without a follow-up plan.
- Do not skip gas testing or permits because the job is urgent.
- Do not assume a part is available until it is confirmed.
- Do not leave the class surveyor out of a class-related repair.
