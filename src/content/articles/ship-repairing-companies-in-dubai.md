When a vessel is off-hire, the cost is not only the repair invoice. It is the missed cargo, the revised schedule, the crew overtime and the commercial pressure that builds with every additional day. Choosing a ship repairing company in Dubai is therefore less about finding the lowest quote and more about finding a team that can mobilise, diagnose and finish without creating a second problem.

Dubai is one of the busiest maritime hubs in the world. Jebel Ali, Dubai Maritime City, Port Rashid and the anchorages around them handle a constant flow of tankers, container ships, bulk carriers, offshore support vessels and tugs. That volume supports a large repair market, but it also means the quality of providers varies widely. Some are full shipyards. Some are specialised technical teams. Others are coordinators who subcontract most of the work.

This guide explains what to compare, what to ask before you award a job, and how to avoid the gaps that turn a straightforward repair into a delayed port call.

## What ship repair actually covers

The phrase "ship repair" is broad. Two companies can both claim the service while offering very different capabilities. Before comparing prices, separate the work into categories:

- **Mechanical and engine work** — main and auxiliary engine maintenance, piston and skirt renewal, valve spindle overhauling, pumps, purifiers and associated machinery.
- **Electrical work** — fault finding, motor rewinding, control panels, sensors and automation interfaces.
- **Steel and fabrication** — onboard welding, structural repairs, brackets, pipe supports and workshop fabrication.
- **Tank cleaning and preparation** — fuel, bilge, heeling and cargo tank cleaning before inspection, repair or a change of cargo.
- **Spares and logistics** — sourcing, clearance and delivery of maker parts, including Caterpillar and Yanmar components.
- **Automation and controls** — engine controls, boiler controls, cargo pump systems, alarms and monitoring.
- **Drydock and hull work** — docking, underwater inspection, coating renewal and larger structural programmes.

A provider that is excellent at engine overhauls may not be the right choice for a cargo tank cleaning sequence. A shipyard may be the right choice for a major drydock but slower for a small electrical fault at anchorage. Start with the scope, then match the provider.

## Why Dubai is a practical repair location

Dubai offers three advantages that matter in a repair situation. First, the port network is dense. A vessel can often be attended at Jebel Ali, Dubai Maritime City, Port Rashid or an anchorage without a long deviation. Second, the supply chain is broad: maker spares, consumables, machining, testing and specialist subcontractors are available locally. Third, the market is competitive, so a well-defined scope usually attracts several credible quotations.

Those advantages disappear if the scope is vague. A quotation based on a two-line email is not a fixed price; it is an estimate with assumptions. The more precisely you describe the fault, the more useful the comparison becomes.

## Eight questions to ask before you award the job

### 1. Can they mobilise to your actual location?

Ask for a mobilisation window for the specific berth, anchorage or yard, not a general "24/7" claim. Confirm who handles port access, gate passes and vessel induction. A fast technical team that cannot get onboard is not fast.

### 2. Is the work delivered in-house or subcontracted?

There is nothing wrong with using specialists, but you should know who is accountable. Ask which trades are in-house, which are subcontracted, and who supervises the complete scope. For multi-trade jobs, a single point of contact reduces the chance of gaps between suppliers.

### 3. Can they identify and source the right parts?

Part delays are one of the most common reasons a repair overruns. Ask whether the provider can source genuine maker parts, what information they need, and how they handle alternatives. For Caterpillar, Yanmar and other major makers, the model, serial number and part number should be confirmed before the vessel is opened up.

### 4. Do they understand the compliance context?

Repair work may touch MARPOL requirements, confined space entry, hot work permits, class surveys or port state control. Ask how the team handles permits, gas testing, waste and documentation. If the job generates oily residues or requires tank entry, the disposal and safety chain should be clear before work starts.

### 5. What does the quotation include?

Compare scope, manning, working hours, equipment, consumables, spare parts, waste disposal, mobilisation and documentation. A lower headline number can become more expensive once these items are added. Ask for assumptions and exclusions in writing.

### 6. How will progress be reported?

For anything longer than a shift, agree a reporting rhythm: daily progress, photographs, parts status and any new findings. The vessel's chief engineer and the shore superintendent should know who to call and when.

### 7. What happens after completion?

Ask for a completion report, photographs, test results and any warranty terms. If the repair affects class or statutory items, confirm what evidence the vessel will retain.

### 8. Can they provide relevant references?

A reference from a similar vessel type or a similar repair is more useful than a generic client list. Ask what work was done, how long it took and whether the provider would be invited back.

## A simple comparison checklist

| Criterion | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Response | Clear mobilisation window for your port and berth |
| Technical depth | Named technicians, relevant maker experience, supervision |
| Parts | Genuine or approved parts with a realistic lead time |
| Port coverage | Attendance at your actual location, not only the yard |
| Compliance | Permits, gas testing, waste handling and documentation |
| Communication | Single coordinator and agreed reporting rhythm |
| Commercials | Itemised scope, assumptions and exclusions |
| Close-out | Completion report, photos, tests and warranty terms |

## The questions that matter most

If you remember only three things, make them these:

1. **Define the scope precisely.** A clear description of the equipment, fault and working window is the foundation of a useful quotation.
2. **Confirm who is actually doing the work.** In-house capability, supervision and accountability matter more than a long service list.
3. **Agree the close-out before you start.** Documentation, tests, photographs and warranty terms should be part of the job, not an afterthought.

A good repair partner does not just fix the immediate fault. They reduce the uncertainty around the next port call.
