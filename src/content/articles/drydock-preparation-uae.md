Drydocking in the UAE is won or lost before the vessel enters the yard. The dock itself is a compressed period of work, and every missing specification, late spare or unclear survey item becomes a delay that the vessel pays for by the day.

A drydock is also a coordination problem. The yard, class, flag, service providers, spare parts suppliers and the vessel's own crew all have tasks that must fit into a short window. When those tasks are sequenced properly, the dock runs calmly. When they are not, the vessel sits on the blocks while someone sources a gasket.

This guide explains how to prepare, what to decide early, and how to leave the yard with a complete record.

## What a drydock actually needs from you

The yard controls the dock, but the vessel's team controls the information. A well-prepared owner or manager arrives with a specification, a defect list, drawings, survey requirements and a clear view of what must be inspected before work is closed up.

The essentials are:

- A written specification organised by area and system
- A current defect and condition list with photographs
- Class and flag requirements, including any postponements
- Long-lead and maker-specific spares identified
- Survey and inspection plans with attendance requirements
- A change-approval process and a cost-control method
- A testing and handover plan

Without these, the yard makes reasonable assumptions, and reasonable assumptions are where overruns begin.

## Build the specification before the yard

The specification is the vessel's instruction to the yard. It should be specific enough to price and clear enough to inspect.

A useful specification states:

1. The area or system to be worked on.
2. The work to be done, including the standard expected.
3. Materials, coatings or parts to be used.
4. The inspection or test that proves completion.
5. The responsible party for each item.
6. Any class or maker requirement that applies.

Photographs help, particularly for hull defects, coating breakdown, piping and structural items. A photograph with a location and a description is far more useful than a generic note.

### Separate mandatory work from desirable work

Class items, safety items and defects that affect seaworthiness are mandatory. Cleaning, cosmetic coating and optional upgrades are not. Keeping the two lists separate protects the schedule when the budget or the dock time comes under pressure.

## Spare parts and long-lead items

Spares are the most common cause of drydock delay. The part that was assumed to be in stock is often the one that needs three weeks. Start with the items that are maker-specific, survey-critical or slow to source.

Plan around these categories:

- Maker-specific components with long lead times
- Parts that need class approval or certification
- Items that are only discovered during inspection
- Consumables that are bulky or hazardous to transport
- Parts that need machining to fit

Confirm lead times in writing and track them against the dock date. If an item will not arrive, decide in advance whether the work is deferred or whether an alternative is acceptable. Leaving that decision until the dock is open is the worst option.

For vessels with several suppliers, [spare parts and logistics support](/services/spare-parts-logistics) can consolidate sourcing and delivery so items arrive in the right sequence.

| Work area | Typical long-lead risk | Prepare by |
|---|---|---|
| Hull and coating | Coating supply and weather | Confirming product and yard slot |
| Propulsion | Maker-specific parts | Ordering against serial numbers |
| Steering gear | Seals and control parts | Inspecting before the dock |
| Deck machinery | Hydraulic components | Checking maker lead times |
| Safety equipment | Certification and servicing | Booking approved providers |
| Piping and valves | Machining and spares | Surveying and listing defects |

## Class, flag and survey coordination

Class and flag requirements shape the dock programme. A survey item that is missed may mean a condition of class, a follow-up attendance or a delay to the vessel's certificates.

Before the dock:

- Confirm which surveys are due and their scope.
- Agree who attends and when.
- Provide the surveyor with access and the records they need.
- Track any condition of class and how it will be cleared.
- Confirm the documentation required for each item.

The vessel's technical manager normally owns this coordination, but the yard and service providers must support it with access, testing and evidence. Agree that division of responsibility in writing.

## The daily rhythm inside the dock

A drydock runs on information. A short daily meeting, a written work list and a visible change log keep the programme honest.

Use a simple routine:

1. Review yesterday's completed and outstanding work.
2. Confirm today's access and permits.
3. Review any new defects found on inspection.
4. Approve or reject scope changes with cost and time impact.
5. Update the critical path and the parts status.
6. Record decisions so they are not revisited later.

Photograph the work as it progresses, especially before areas are closed up or coated. Those images are valuable evidence for class, warranty and future maintenance.

## Testing, sea trials and handover

A drydock is not complete when the last item is painted. It is complete when the systems have been tested and the records are handed over.

Agree in advance:

- Which systems require dock testing
- Which require sea trials
- Who attends and who signs off
- What measurements or certificates are required
- How outstanding items will be tracked after departure

The handover pack should include the work done, parts fitted, measurements, test results, certificates, as-built changes and a clear list of any outstanding items with owners and dates.

## Mistakes and limits

Common mistakes include arriving without a specification, ordering parts after the dock opens, failing to separate mandatory from optional work, approving verbal changes, and leaving without a complete record.

There are real limits. Weather can affect hull work, inspections can reveal more than expected, and some maker parts cannot be rushed. A good preparation plan includes contingency for the likely discoveries rather than assuming none will occur.

## Choosing and preparing the right support

The yard is only one partner. Technical service providers, coating specialists, automation engineers and parts suppliers all contribute to the dock programme. Choose providers who can work to the specification, report daily and coordinate with the yard rather than around it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How early should I prepare for drydocking in the UAE?

Start planning several months ahead where possible. The specification, class items, long-lead spares and yard slot all need lead time, and late changes are expensive once the vessel is in dock.

### What should a drydock specification include?

It should list the work by area, the standard required, the responsible party, the materials and the inspection points. Clear specifications reduce disputes and help the yard plan labour.

### How do I manage long-lead spare parts?

Identify maker-specific and survey-critical items early, confirm lead times in writing and track them against the dock date. Have a plan for any item that will not arrive in time.

### Who coordinates class and flag surveys?

The vessel's technical manager usually coordinates with class and flag, while the yard and service providers support with access, testing and records. Agree responsibilities before the dock.

### What happens at the end of a drydock?

The vessel should complete testing, sea trials where required, and a documented handover covering work done, measurements, certificates and outstanding items.
